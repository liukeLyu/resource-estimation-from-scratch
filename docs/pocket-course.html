The exact answer here is known from free fermions. Why estimate a quantum computation for it at all?

To calibrate: every factor can be checked on a model you understand before trusting the same pipeline on a molecule nobody can solve.

In a surface code, Clifford gates (H, S, CNOT, Pauli measurements) are done by moving and measuring patches of qubits. They cost time and space, but no special resource.

A non-Clifford gate needs a magic state, made in a factory of thousands of physical qubits over tens of error-correction rounds. So, to first approximation, an algorithm's cost is its count of non-Clifford gates: T gates, or Toffolis (one CCZ state each).

Exchange rate: a Toffoli onto a fresh \(|0\rangle\) (a "temporary AND") costs 4 T gates [Gidney, arXiv:1709.06648].

A circuit has 106 CNOTs and 103 Toffolis. Which number sets its fault-tolerant cost?

The 103 Toffolis: each needs a magic state from a factory; CNOTs are Clifford.

Every estimate in this course has the same shape:

\(\text{Toffolis} = \left\lceil \dfrac{\pi\lambda}{2\epsilon}\right\rceil \times \big(\text{SELECT} + 2\,\text{PREPARE} + \text{reflection}\big)\)

The first factor is the number of steps of a quantum walk that phase estimation runs [Lee et al., arXiv:2011.03494, Eq. 45]. \(\lambda = \sum_l |c_l|\) is the one-norm of \(H = \sum_l c_l P_l\). The bracket is the cost of one walk step; parts 2 to 4 build each piece.

It doubles (the walk calls double). But the energies doubled too, so at the same relative accuracy nothing changed.

A circuit \(A\) succeeds with probability \(a = \sin^2\theta\): \(A|0\rangle = \sin\theta|\text{good}\rangle + \cos\theta|\text{bad}\rangle\). Two reflections, one about \(|\text{bad}\rangle\) and one about \(A|0\rangle\), make a rotation by \(2\theta\) in that plane.

\(Q = -A\,S_0\,A^\dagger\,S_\text{good}, \qquad Q^m A|0\rangle: \ \text{good amplitude } \sin((2m+1)\theta)\)

Three qubits, one marked state out of eight: \(a = 1/8\).

In the code: qcost.amplify.success_after(A, good, m).

Your dataSuccess 0.125, 0.781, 0.945 after 0, 1, 2 rounds; then 0.330 after 3 (overshoot). Notebook 1.

Why does a third round make things worse?

The rotation keeps going past \(\pi/2\): \((2m+1)\theta\) overshoots the good axis.

Repeating until success takes \(1/a\) tries. Amplification needs about \(\pi/(4\theta)\) rounds, and for small \(a\), \(\theta \approx \sqrt a\): about \(1/\sqrt a\) rounds [Brassard, Høyer, Mosca, Tapp, quant-ph/0005055, Thm. 2].

The catch: you must know \(a\) to stop at the right round.

Your data\(a = 10^{-4}\): 78 rounds against 10 000 tries; \(a = 10^{-6}\): 785 against 106. Notebook 1.

Each round uses \(A\) twice. At \(a = 10^{-4}\), how many uses of \(A\) in all?

About 157 (one to start, two per round), still about 60 times fewer than 10 000 tries.

A block encoding \(U\) applies an operator \(V\) when its ancilla is found in \(|0\rangle\): \(U|0\rangle|\psi\rangle = \sin\theta\,|0\rangle V|\psi\rangle + \dots\). Plain amplification would reflect about \(U|0\rangle|\psi\rangle\), which needs \(|\psi\rangle\).

If \(V\) is unitary, \(\sin\theta\) is the same for every \(|\psi\rangle\), and reflecting about the ancilla alone works: one round is \(-URU^\dagger RU\) [Berry et al., arXiv:1312.1414, Lemma 3.6]. At \(\sin\theta = 1/2\) one round is exact.

Your data\(V = e^{i\varphi X}\) as an LCU with coefficients summing to 2: success 0.250 before, 1.000 after one round, and the state is \(V|\psi\rangle\) for every random input. Notebook 1.

Why pad the LCU so its coefficients sum to exactly 2?

So the block is \(V/2\): \(\sin\theta = 1/2\), \(\theta = \pi/6\), and one round reaches \(3\theta = \pi/2\).

A block encoding of \(H/\lambda\) is not a unitary times a constant. Its success amplitude \(\|H|\psi\rangle\|/\lambda\) depends on \(|\psi\rangle\), so no fixed number of rounds suits every input.

That is why phase estimation runs the walk operator itself, whose eigenphases carry the energy (part 4), instead of trying to apply \(H\).

Your data3-spin TFIM block encoding: success amplitude 0.680 on the ground state, 0.463 on a random state. Notebook 1.

For which states would the amplitude be exactly 1?

Eigenstates with \(|E| = \lambda\), which happens only if every term acts with full weight in the same direction: almost never.

A Toffoli onto a qubit known to be \(|0\rangle\) computes \(t = a\wedge b\) for 4 T gates. Undoing it is free: measure \(t\) in the X basis; on outcome 1, apply CZ between \(a\) and \(b\) to fix the sign \((-1)^{ab}\) [Gidney, arXiv:1709.06648, Fig. 3].

So every "compute, use, uncompute" pattern pays once. This one trick halves most published costs.

In the code: Circuit.and_ counts one Toffoli, Circuit.unand counts zero.

Your dataOn a 3-qubit state vector, both outcomes leave the controls exactly as they were. Notebook 2.

What would uncomputing by running the Toffoli again cost?

Another Toffoli. Measurement plus a Clifford fix-up costs none.

Adder, \(b \leftarrow a + b \bmod 2^n\): the carry into bit \(i+1\) is one AND after two CNOTs, \(c_{i+1} = (a_i\oplus c_i)(b_i\oplus c_i)\oplus c_i\). Ripple up computing carries, ripple down undoing them for free: \(n - 1\) Toffolis.

Comparator, flag \(a < b\): the carry out of \((2^n - 1 - a) + b\) is 1 exactly when \(b > a\). Compute only the carries (\(n\) ANDs), copy the last, undo by measurement: \(n\) Toffolis.

In the code: qcost.arithmetic.adder(n), comparator(n); Circuit.run checks every input.

Block encodings look things up: a term's weight, a rotation angle. A QROM maps \(|l\rangle|0\rangle \to |l\rangle|d_l\rangle\) for a classical table \(d_0,\dots,d_{L-1}\), for all \(l\) at once.

Writing a word is free: once a flag qubit says "the address is \(l\)", CNOTs copy the 1-bits of \(d_l\). The only cost is computing the flags.

In the code: qcost.qrom.qrom(data, b).

Your dataFive 6-bit words [13, 2, 61, 40, 7]: every address returns its word, with 3 Toffolis. Notebook 3.

Does the cost change if the words are 64 bits instead of 6?

No: extra bits are extra CNOTs, which are Clifford.

The flags are nodes of a binary tree over the address bits. A left child is "parent AND next bit is 0": one temporary AND. Its right sibling is parent XOR left child: one CNOT. Undoing the AND is free.

So each node with two children costs one Toffoli, and a tree with \(L\) leaves has \(L - 1\) of them [Babbush et al., arXiv:1805.03662, Fig. 7: \(4L - 4\) T]. Without a control, the root's flag is just the top address bit: one fewer.

Your data\(L = 100\): 98 Toffolis uncontrolled, 99 controlled, for 1-bit and 32-bit words alike. Notebook 3.

SELECT applies \(P_l\) controlled on the flag of leaf \(l\). Toffolis over \(L\) terms?

\(L - 1\): same tree, and controlled Paulis are Clifford.

\(L\) Toffolis is a lot for a long table of short words. Split the address: the high bits pick a block of \(k\) words, written all at once into \(k\) registers (free per bit), and the low bits drive \(k - 1\) controlled swaps of \(b\)-bit words that bring the wanted one to the front [Low, Kliuchnikov, Schaeffer, arXiv:1812.00954].

\(\text{Toffolis} = \lceil L/k\rceil + b(k-1)\)

[Berry et al., arXiv:1902.02134, App. A]. The price: \(b(k-1)\) more qubits.

In the code: qcost.qrom.select_swap_qrom(data, b, k).

Your data\(L = 37\), \(b = 4\): 35, 21, 20, 31, 61 Toffolis for \(k = 1, 2, 4, 8, 16\), equal to PennyLane's QROM each time. Notebook 3.

Why does \(k = 16\) cost more than \(k = 1\) here?

The swap term \(b(k-1) = 60\) outgrows what it saves on a table of only 37 words.

The two terms balance when \(L/k \approx bk\): \(k \approx \sqrt{L/b}\), and the cost drops from \(L\) to about \(2\sqrt{Lb}\).

Undoing a lookup by running it backwards costs as much again. Measuring the output in the X basis instead leaves a sign on each address, fixed by a one-bit lookup: \(\lceil L/k\rceil + k\) Toffolis, best at \(k \approx \sqrt L\) [Berry et al., App. C].

PREPARE must load \(\sum_l \sqrt{|c_l|/\lambda}\,|l\rangle\). The classical alias method cuts any distribution over \(L\) items into \(L\) equal buckets of at most two items each: item \(l\) keeps \(\text{keep}_l/2^\mu\) of its bucket, the rest goes to \(\text{alt}_l\).

To sample: pick a bucket uniformly, pick \(\sigma < 2^\mu\) uniformly, output \(l\) if \(\sigma < \text{keep}_l\), else \(\text{alt}_l\).

In the code: qcost.alias.alias_tables(weights, mu).

Run the same recipe on superpositions [Babbush et al., arXiv:1805.03662, Fig. 11]: uniform superpositions over \(l\) and \(\sigma\); a QROM writes \((\text{keep}_l, \text{alt}_l)\) (\(L - 1\)); a comparator (\(\mu\)); a controlled swap of \(l\) with \(\text{alt}_l\) (\(\lceil\log_2 L\rceil\)).

Every TFIM coupling equals 1 and every field equals \(h\). What could a smarter PREPARE do?

Skip the table: one rotation to split bonds from sites, then uniform superpositions. Almost free.

PREPARE weights the terms; SELECT applies them, \(\sum_l |l\rangle\langle l| \otimes \text{sign}(c_l)P_l\). Together:

\(\langle 0|\,\text{PREP}^\dagger\,\text{SELECT}\,\text{PREP}\,|0\rangle = H/\lambda\)

The weight \(\sqrt{|c_l|/\lambda}\) appears twice, once from PREP and once from PREP\(^\dagger\), giving \(|c_l|/\lambda\). That is why \(\lambda\), not the number of terms, is the normalization.

Why does SELECT carry \(\text{sign}(c_l)\)?

PREP can only load magnitudes \(\sqrt{|c_l|}\); the sign has to come from the unitary.

Multiply by the reflection \(R = 2|0\rangle\langle 0| - 1\) on the index register. The walk \(W = R\,\text{PREP}^\dagger\,\text{SELECT}\,\text{PREP}\) has eigenvalues \(e^{\pm i\arccos(E_k/\lambda)}\) for every eigenvalue \(E_k\) of \(H\) [Low and Chuang, arXiv:1610.06546].

Why: for each eigenvector, \(W\) acts on a 2D plane as a rotation by \(\arccos(E_k/\lambda)\), the picture of lesson 6 again.

In the code: qcost.walk.walk_matrix(H).

Your data4-spin TFIM: \(W\) is \(128\times128\); every \(\arccos(E_k/\lambda)\) is a phase of \(W\) to \(4\times10^{-16}\); 32 phases carry energies, the other 96 are \(\pm1\) on states a start from \(|0\rangle|\psi\rangle\) never reaches. Notebook 4.

How many walk eigenvalues does one energy \(E_k\) give?

Two: \(e^{+i\arccos(E_k/\lambda)}\) and \(e^{-i\arccos(E_k/\lambda)}\).

\(|d\arccos(E/\lambda)/dE| \ge 1/\lambda\), so an energy error \(\epsilon\) needs a phase error about \(\epsilon/\lambda\), and phase estimation needs a number of walk steps proportional to \(\lambda/\epsilon\).

Two conventions are in use. Lee et al. need \(\lceil\pi\lambda/(2\epsilon)\rceil\) [Eq. 45]. Babbush et al. define \(\epsilon\) as a root-mean-square error and need \(\sqrt2\pi\lambda/\epsilon\) [arXiv:1805.03662, Eq. 26]. The ratio is \(2\sqrt2 \approx 2.8\).

Your data8×8 Hubbard, \(\epsilon = 0.01\): \(2.27\times10^5\) calls in the RMS convention, \(8.04\times10^4\) in Lee et al.'s. Notebook 7.

Two papers report costs a factor 2.8 apart for the same circuit. First thing to check?

How each defines the energy error \(\epsilon\).

A walk step is SELECT, PREPARE, PREPARE\(^\dagger\) and the reflection. The reflection acts on the \(\lceil\log_2 L\rceil\) index qubits and the \(\mu\) qubits of \(\sigma\), with one more control for phase estimation; a NOT with \(n\) controls costs \(n - 1\) Toffolis.

For a generic Pauli sum that is about \(3L\): \(L\) for SELECT and \(L\) for each PREPARE.

Double factorization uses fewer Toffolis but more qubits. Where do the qubits go?

Into the select/swap lookup of rotation angles, which trades qubits for Toffolis.

Group indices in pairs: \(V_{(pq),(rs)} = (pq|rs)\) is a positive semidefinite \(n^2\times n^2\) matrix. Its eigendecomposition gives \((pq|rs) = \sum_{\ell=1}^{L} W^\ell_{pq}W^\ell_{rs}\): the two-body part becomes a sum of \(L\) squares of one-body operators.

Each \(W^\ell\) is symmetric, so it has its own orbitals: \(W^\ell = U^\ell\,\text{diag}(f^\ell)\,U^{\ell T}\). In those orbitals a square is number operators. The circuit: rotate orbitals, apply \(Z\)s, rotate back [von Burg et al., arXiv:2007.14460].

Keep eigenvector \(m\) if \(\big(\sum_p|f^\ell_p|\big)|f^\ell_m|\) exceeds a threshold [Lee et al., Eq. C41].

In the code: qcost.factorization.double_factorize(eri, thresh).

A logical qubit is a \(d\times d\) patch, about \(2(d+1)^2\) physical qubits [Gidney and Ekerå, arXiv:1905.09749]. It fails per round with

\(p_L = 0.1\,(100\,p)^{(d+1)/2}\)

[Fowler and Gidney, arXiv:1808.06709]. At \(p = 10^{-3}\), every 2 added to \(d\) divides \(p_L\) by 10. Pick the smallest odd \(d\) with (patches) × (rounds) × \(p_L\) below the failure budget.

Of the 3 725 logical qubits, the select/swap output registers for angles take \(k_r N\beth/2\) with \(k_r = 4\). The system itself is 108.

Lee et al. also sum their step list into one formula, Eq. C39. The step list gives \(8\aleph - 42\) for the non-lookup parts; Eq. C39 has \(9\aleph - 43\). The published totals follow the step list. A reproduction finds such things; a reading does not.

Your dataAngle registers 3 456 of 3 725 qubits. Eq. C39: 21 762 per step against the step list's 21 753, a difference \(\aleph - 1 = 9\). Notebook 8.

Plain QROMs (\(k = 1\)) instead of select/swap: more or fewer qubits, more or fewer Toffolis?

Far fewer qubits (no 3 456-qubit angle block), far more Toffolis (about 13 000 per lookup instead of 5 850).

Raise the threshold and fewer eigenvectors survive: fewer angles to load, slightly smaller \(\lambda\), a less accurate Hamiltonian. The cost moves slowly, because \(\lambda\) barely changes and lookups grow like \(\sqrt{L\Xi}\).

So the threshold is set by accuracy, not cost.

Your dataFeMoco: threshold \(10^{-4}\) gives \(1.35\times10^{10}\) Toffolis (25 054 eigenvectors), \(1.25\times10^{-3}\) gives \(1.01\times10^{10}\), \(10^{-2}\) gives \(6.76\times10^9\) (4 700). A factor 2 over two orders of magnitude. Notebook 8.

\(\lambda\) is 296.7 at threshold \(10^{-4}\) and 283.4 at \(10^{-2}\). What carries most of the factor 2 in cost?

The lookups (5 times fewer eigenvectors), not \(\lambda\) (only 5% smaller).
